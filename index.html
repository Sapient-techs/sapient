Read the longer story

I can’t remember when I fell in love with tech. It has always been part of who I am. growing up, tech was like a bowl filled with different candies that you love, you always want a taste of everything.

It started with repairing torchlights and pulling the DC motors out of dead CD players to build fans. Then came RC cars, and cardboard boxes became car bodies, VR goggles, periscopes and racing-game controllers for my phone.

At some point I moved from physical tech to digital, and the digital world felt unreal. There was so much to pick from: game dev, web dev, app dev and more, and I wanted to touch all of it. I tried learning Java through a gamified app, but I never finished it, and I wish I had stayed focused. Then I found Sketchware, one of the first drag-and-drop development tools. It ran on Java, so it was Java with blocks, and its Facebook community was legendary. What you could build was limited only by your creativity and skill: apps, games and more. It made me feel like a developer. When its founders shut it down for lack of funding, community members built Sketchware Pro, with more features and more bugs, and I loved that too.

Then I found Unity, and Struckd, a lighter alternative that worked well on my phone, and I built plenty of 3D games with it. Everything I did back then was on my itel A14. I also used AIDE and Pocket Code.

Then I went into cybersecurity and found Zanti, a mobile penetration-testing app that security professionals use to analyse a network and find its weaknesses. It fascinated me that a tool like that could fit on a phone.

Later I got into WordPress, back when it wasn’t as popular as it is today, playing with themes and templates. Then animation: I made cartoons with Plotagon, which lagged so badly my phone almost gave up.

Then I found Python. I taught myself from YouTube, and it was rough because I didn’t have my own PC. I used Pydroid 3 and built a speech-recognition bot with speech-to-text and text-to-speech, plus date and time, random and other modules. I tried Termux too.

I can’t remember a time I wasn’t doing something related to tech, and I have always dreamed of building products and services. The joy of people using what I make is almost enough to keep me going. When I found trading, I couldn’t stop thinking about joining the two worlds. That is why most of my projects today merge smart systems and AI with trading, and why I chose Python. Now I’m going deep into machine learning and AI.

What I’m building toward

  • A smart trading system of my own.
  • A tech product or service that lots of people use.
  • Leaving the world with a positive mark and influence.

 

Reading charts by hand

I started where most quants do: staring at price. Smart Money Concepts became my language for it. I published signals at Echelon Forex Academy, moderated the Crypto Tiger community, and wrote MetaTrader 5 Expert Advisors to automate what I was doing by eye.

SapientSignals

A signal reporting bridge that sends MetaTrader 5 output to a Node.js dashboard.

The drawdown

Not everything worked, and the curve shows it. I keep the losing trades because they changed how I build.

Sporty Hero advisor

A Python tool that ran statistical analysis on historical crash-game multipliers. It flopped. The maths was fine, but the premise wasn’t: a game built to be unpredictable doesn’t reward a better model of its past. Now I check that a signal can exist before I build the thing that measures it.

Teaching machines to explain price

The pivot: stop reading charts alone and make software that can read them, then say why.

APEX

Autonomous Price Explainer. A Smart Money Concepts quantization engine that turns chart structure into price reads you can inspect.

TradeVault

A SaaS for visual search across trading screenshots, so you can find an old setup by what it looked like.
